# 02 Studio Las Method

## Purpose

This document defines the Studio Las Method.

It describes how the trainer thinks, interprets signals, and makes decisions.

It is not a training plan.

It is not an app workflow.

It is not a library of exercises.

The method is the decision logic behind the Studio Las client process.

## Core principle

Studio Las Method is guided interpretation.

The trainer does not simply deliver workouts.

The trainer observes the person, forms a working hypothesis, applies the smallest useful intervention, watches the signal, and adjusts the direction.

The method exists to help the client regain functional capacity, trust in the body, and long-term independence.

## Method doctrine

A good Studio Las decision is not the hardest decision.

It is not the most advanced decision.

It is not the most data-rich decision.

A good Studio Las decision is the next decision that is safe, meaningful, and useful for this person now.

The method values:

- clarity over complexity,
- interpretation over tracking,
- progression over intensity,
- safety over ambition,
- independence over engagement,
- calm repetition over novelty,
- trainer judgment over automation.

## The trainer's primary question

The trainer should repeatedly ask:

> What does this person need next to move with more trust and less chaos?

This question has priority over:

- what exercise is popular,
- what the app can record,
- what the client expected to do,
- what looks impressive,
- what creates more data,
- what increases engagement.

## The Studio Las decision loop

The method follows a simple decision loop:

1. Observe  
   See the person, not only the movement.

2. Interpret  
   Ask what the signal might mean in this context.

3. Hypothesize  
   Define the most likely current limitation or opportunity.

4. Choose  
   Select the smallest useful next step.

5. Apply  
   Run the intervention in a controlled way.

6. Watch  
   Observe body response, confidence, tolerance, pain, fatigue, and understanding.

7. Adjust  
   Repeat, regress, pause, simplify, progress, or redirect.

8. Record  
   Preserve only the signal that may matter later.

9. Review  
   Convert repeated observations into a pattern.

10. Decide again  
   Use the pattern to choose the next direction.

## What the trainer observes

The trainer observes more than performance.

Relevant signals may include:

- pain behavior,
- movement quality,
- hesitation,
- breath holding,
- facial tension,
- fear of a position,
- asymmetry,
- fatigue response,
- recovery between efforts,
- confidence before and after a task,
- ability to understand instructions,
- consistency between sessions,
- client language about the body,
- daily-life function.

Not every observation needs to become data.

Only observations that may change a future decision should be recorded.

## How the trainer forms hypotheses

A hypothesis is a temporary explanation, not a diagnosis.

Examples:

- The client avoids loading the knee because they expect pain.
- The client lacks strength more than mobility.
- The client needs simpler home guidance because cognitive load is too high.
- The client tolerates movement well in session but loses confidence alone.
- The current plan is too ambitious for recovery capacity.
- The issue may require medical or physiotherapy input before progression.

A hypothesis should be useful enough to guide the next step, but humble enough to be changed.

## When to progress

The trainer may progress when:

- the client understands the task,
- pain response is acceptable and understood,
- movement quality is stable enough,
- recovery is adequate,
- fear decreases or remains manageable,
- the client can repeat the task without excessive reassurance,
- the progression supports the current goal.

Progression can mean more load, more range, more complexity, more autonomy, or more responsibility.

Progression does not always mean harder training.

## When to regress

The trainer should regress when:

- fear increases,
- pain response becomes unclear or disproportionate,
- technique collapses under load,
- the client cannot reproduce the task safely,
- recovery is poor,
- the client becomes more confused,
- the home task is not being done because it is too difficult or unclear.

Regression is not failure.

Regression protects continuity.

## When to pause

The trainer should pause progression when:

- the signal is ambiguous,
- the client's condition changes,
- symptoms require external review,
- the client is under unusual stress or fatigue,
- the risk of pushing exceeds the value of pushing,
- the trainer needs more information.

A pause is an active decision.

It prevents the process from becoming reckless.

## When to simplify

The trainer should simplify when the client has too many instructions, too much fear, too many competing goals, or too much app/paper/process friction.

Simplification may include:

- fewer exercises,
- shorter home guidance,
- one clear daily anchor,
- one signal to report,
- one priority for the week,
- less measurement.

Studio Las should not confuse seriousness with complexity.

## When to refer out

Studio Las Method must respect its boundary.

The trainer should recommend external medical, physiotherapy, psychological, or specialist input when the situation is outside the trainer-led process.

The trainer does not use Studio Las OS to diagnose.

The trainer does not use the method to replace healthcare.

The safe decision may be to stop, refer, or wait.

## Role of paper

Paper carries the morning because it protects the client from starting the day in the app.

Paper should contain only what the client needs to do calmly and independently.

Paper is not a productivity system.

Paper is a quiet guide.

## Role of the app

The app records the signal after the offline action.

It should not become the ritual.

It should not ask the client to manage the method.

It should not produce automated meaning for the client.

The app supports trainer memory and future pattern recognition.

## Role of the report

The report turns accumulated signals and trainer interpretation into a clear decision point.

The report should answer:

- what we learned,
- what changed,
- what still limits the client,
- what is now safe to do,
- what should happen next.

## Anti-method

Studio Las Method is not:

- pushing intensity because the client paid,
- collecting every possible metric,
- changing exercises for novelty,
- using motivation to override fear,
- automating trainer judgment,
- treating completion as success,
- treating pain as weakness,
- turning home guidance into a habit tracker.

## Implication for Studio Las OS

Studio Las OS must encode support for trainer decisions, not replace them.

Before implementing any feature, define:

1. What trainer decision does this support?
2. What signal is required?
3. Who creates the meaning?
4. Where should the client encounter the guidance: paper, conversation, app, or report?
5. What should not be recorded?

If the feature cannot answer these questions, it is not ready for implementation.
